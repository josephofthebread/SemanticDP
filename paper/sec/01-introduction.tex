\section{Introduction}
Organizations,
wishing to train their large language models (LLMs) on the sensitive data records are expected to do so under a formal privacy guarantee.
The standard answers are private optimization techniques, which add calibrated noise to the gradient updates \citep{abadi2016deep, mironov2017renyi},
and text sanitization,
which alters the training data itself before the model even sees it \citep{feyisetan2020privacy, yue2021differential, carvalho2021tem}.
Both are mature enough to deploy,
and both are routinely evaluated the same way:
fit the model at several privacy budgets,
report benchmark accuracy,
and read off the cost of privacy as the gap to a non-private baseline.

What privacy costs is not only how often the model is right,
but how it behaves.
An adapted model can keep its benchmark accuracy and still behave worse,
e.g. follow instructions less reliably,
stops refusing requests it should decline and etc.
We call this alignment degradation.
That privacy induces it is established:
\citet{ramesh2026privacyhallucination} fine-tune under private optimization and find factual consistency dropping as the budget tightens,
with the damage falling on facts learned during fine-tuning rather than on what the model already knew.
The cause is open,
and it decides the fix.
If noise destabilizes optimization,
the fix is a better optimizer.
If the damage runs through the meaning of the training text,
then any mechanism that distorts meaning will cause it,
private or not,
and the fix must protect meaning instead.

We therefore measure how much each mechanism changes the meaning of the training text:
the embedding distance between the original text and what the mechanism produced.
That gives one scale on which private optimization and sanitization can be compared,
even though their privacy budgets cannot be.
We then add a mechanism with no privacy guarantee at all,
random token replacement,
which damages meaning without buying any privacy.
If degradation follows distortion there too,
then it is the damage to meaning that matters,
not the privacy.
We run every mechanism on two datasets that differ in how many facts their text carries:
entity-rich and entity-poor.

\paragraph{What we find.}
Distortion predicts degradation;
the privacy budget barely does.
Tightening the private optimiser by more than an order of magnitude leaves measured degradation where it was,
and sanitization does its damage as a cliff rather than a gradient,
with most of its budget range carrying no information about how the model will behave.
The sharpest result is a reversal:
damaging the language around the facts costs far more than damaging the facts themselves,
so a mechanism aimed only at the sensitive spans keeps refusal and factual grounding intact where uniform sanitization destroys both.
The privacy mechanism was aimed at the sensitive content;
what it actually damaged was the model's ability to behave.
The study uses three small instruction-tuned open-weight models of one to two billion parameters,
two instruction datasets,
low-rank adapters and three seeds per condition.
